The Jar Method: How to Stop Dismissing the Good in Someone

By Hazik Fayaz · August 13, 2026

I was having a conversation with one of my clients.

She was having problems with her partner in one specific area. And because of that one problem, she was dismissing all the other attributes — the good things — in that person entirely.

One flaw was erasing everything else.

So I gave her a method I discovered myself: The Jar Method.

Here's how it works:

Imagine every good attribute, every good characteristic a person has — as a cookie.

Now imagine jars. Her jar. His jar.

Every good quality he has goes into his jar, as a cookie. Every good quality she has goes into her jar, as a cookie.

When she thinks about him, the cookies are sitting right there, available in the jar.

She can reach in and take one whenever she wants.

Kindness — a cookie.
Loyalty — a cookie.
The way he shows up for her family — a cookie.
His humor, his effort, his consistency — cookies, all sitting in the jar.

But here's the problem she was having:

She had one difficult area with him — one jar she wasn't comfortable with, one part of the relationship that felt restricted, limited, or empty.

And instead of seeing that as one jar out of many, she was treating it as if all the jars were empty.

As if none of the cookies existed anymore.

This is what dismissing does.

One problem area. One uncomfortable jar. And suddenly, every good quality — every cookie in every other jar — becomes invisible.

You forget they're even there.

The Jar Method fixes this by making the good visible again — literally.

If one jar isn't accessible right now — if there's a boundary, a restriction, a "not yet" — that's okay.

You don't empty every other jar because of one.

You say: This jar has a time frame. This jar isn't available right now. But look — the other jars still have cookies in them.

Why this works:

Our minds are wired to focus on what's missing, not what's present.

One unmet need, one point of friction, one uncomfortable conversation — and we start acting like the whole relationship is empty.

But it's not. It's just one jar.

How to use this for yourself:

  1. List the jars. What are the different areas of this relationship — communication, effort, respect, humor, support, intimacy, reliability?
  2. Fill them with cookies. For each jar, name what's actually there. Be honest — don't skip the good ones just because you're upset.
  3. Identify the empty or restricted jar. Which one is actually the problem right now? Name it specifically instead of letting it bleed into everything else.
  4. Set a time frame if needed. If a jar isn't accessible right now — communicate that. "Not right now" is different from "never" and different from "nothing is here."
  5. Check the other jars before you conclude the relationship is broken. Are they actually empty? Or did you stop looking because one jar hurt?

This isn't about ignoring real problems.

If a jar is genuinely empty — if there's no respect, no effort, no reciprocity in an area that matters deeply to you — that's real, and it needs to be addressed.

But most of the time, one difficult jar doesn't mean all the jars are empty.

It means you have one conversation to have. One boundary to set. One area to work on.

Not a reason to throw out every good thing sitting in every other jar.

What I told my client:

You're allowed to be upset about one jar.

You're allowed to set a boundary, ask for change, even restrict access to that jar until things improve.

But don't convince yourself the other jars are empty when they're not.

Go back and look. The cookies are still there.

To anyone struggling in one area of a relationship:

Before you decide the whole thing is broken — go jar by jar.

Name what's actually missing.
Name what's actually still there.

You might find you have far more cookies than you realized.

You just stopped looking in the right jars.
